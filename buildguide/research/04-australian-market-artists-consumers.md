# The Australian Art Market: Artists and Consumers

*Research compiled 30 August 2026 for AccessArt — an online marketplace (web + mobile) for renting, buying and exchanging original art directly from emerging artists, launching in South East Queensland (SEQ).*

**Executive summary.** Australia's visual arts economy is structurally lopsided: consumer engagement with the arts is at record highs — 98% of Australians engage with arts and creativity and 74% attended live arts events in 2025 ([Creative Australia, NAPS 2025](https://creative.gov.au/news-events/news/more-australians-attending-arts-ever-cost-growing-barrier)) — yet the ~47,100 practising professional artists earn on average just $23,200 a year from creative work ([Creative Australia, *Artists as Workers*, 2024](https://creative.gov.au/research/artists-workers-economic-study-professional-artists-australia)), and visual/craft artists specifically averaged only $13,937 from their practice in 2023–24 ([RMIT/University of Melbourne, 2025](https://www.rmit.edu.au/news/all-news/2025/apr/arts-workers)). The traditional intermediary — the commercial gallery — is contracting, with a wave of closures through 2024–25 ([ArtsHub](https://www.artshub.com.au/news/features/art-market-lessons-learned-in-2024-2770511/)), while direct-to-consumer channels like Bluethumb (110,000+ works sold, 20,000 artists) are growing ([Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/life/collectibles/digital-marketplace-bluethumb-paints-australian-artists-a-50-million-picture/)). Cost is the #1 barrier to arts participation (60% of Australians — [NAPS 2025](https://creative.gov.au/news-events/news/more-australians-attending-arts-ever-cost-growing-barrier)), which is precisely the problem a rent/buy/exchange model attacks. SEQ is Australia's fastest-growing region — on track for 4.5 million people by the 2032 Brisbane Olympics and 6 million by 2046 ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/au/en/media/media-releases/2025/12/south-east-queensland-population-soars.html)) — with ~35% of Greater Brisbane households renting ([ABS Census 2021](https://abs.gov.au/census/find-census-data/quickstats/2021/3GBRI)), a natural audience for art rental over permanent purchase.

## 1. Market size: Australia's visual arts economy

**Sector revenue.** IBISWorld sizes the Australian *art galleries and museums* industry at **$2.5 billion in 2024** (down 0.3% year-on-year), forecast to reach **~$2.6 billion by 2025–26** at ~0.4% annualised growth ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/au/market-size/art-galleries-museums/); [2026 update](https://www.ibisworld.com/australia/market-size/art-galleries-and-museums/644/)). Note this includes publicly funded museums, so it overstates the commercial art-sales market; it is best used as a ceiling figure.

**Secondary (auction) market.** Reported total Australian art auction sales were approximately **A$141.6m (2023), A$136.1m (2024) and A$149.5m (2025)** ([ArtVals, Global Art Market Trends 2026](https://www.artvals.com/news/global-art-market-trends-2026)). Indigenous art auctions recorded **A$10.4m in Australian sales in H1 2025** (A$11.7m including overseas) ([D'Lan Contemporary Mid-Year Report 2025](https://dlancontemporary.com.au/mid-year-art-market-report-2025/)). The auction market is flat-to-modest — relevant to AccessArt mainly as sentiment signal, since emerging-artist primary sales barely touch it.

**Household spending (ABS — dated, flag).** The most recent granular ABS figure is old: the 2009–10 Household Expenditure Survey recorded **$1,042m total annual household spending on visual arts and crafts** (average **$2.38/week per household**), including **$547m on paintings, carvings and sculptures** ([ABS 4172.0, Arts and Culture in Australia: A Statistical Overview, 2014](https://www.abs.gov.au/ausstats/abs@.nsf/Lookup/4172.0main+features212014)). That was already a 26% real increase on 2003–04 ($670m). *Author note: indexed only for CPI and household growth, this implies plausibly $1.5–2bn+ of annual household visual arts/craft spend today, but no current official figure exists — treat any updated number as an estimate.*

**Commercial gallery sector health: contracting.** ArtsHub's 2024 review documents a wave of closures: Artereal Gallery (Sydney, closing 2025), Cement Fondu (Sydney), Gagprojects (Adelaide), The Main Gallery (Adelaide), Gallery Pompom (Sydney), RAFT Artspace (Alice Springs) and The Strand Gallery (Port Elliot) all closed in/around 2024, with dealers reporting exhibitions "no longer sell themselves" and a return to conservative buying ([ArtsHub, "Art market lessons learned in 2024"](https://www.artshub.com.au/news/features/art-market-lessons-learned-in-2024-2770511/)). Gallery real-estate costs are pushing the "white cube" model out of reach, and dealer representation of artists is being restructured ([ArtsHub, "The changing landscape of artist/dealer representation"](https://www.artshub.com.au/news/opinions-analysis/the-changing-landscape-of-artist-dealer-representation-2764668/)). An older but structurally relevant analysis (2019 — flag as pre-COVID) argued the Australian art market had "flatlined", with the middle market hollowing out ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/friday-essay-the-australian-art-market-has-flatlined-what-can-be-done-to-revive-it-122932)). **Implication: the incumbent channel for emerging artists is shrinking just as demand-side engagement peaks.**

**Online marketplace benchmark.** Bluethumb (Adelaide-founded, Australia's largest online art marketplace) has sold **110,000+ original artworks**, lists **~$306m of art** from **~20,000 Australian artists**, reaches an audience of 500,000+, and reports average cart size **tripling over ten years**; cumulative artist sales passed the ~$50m milestone ([Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/life/collectibles/digital-marketplace-bluethumb-paints-australian-artists-a-50-million-picture/); [Bluethumb](https://bluethumb.com.au/); [Bluethumb partner page](https://bluethumb.com.au/partner)). This is proof of Australian willingness to buy original art online at scale.

**Art rental precedent.** The federal government's **Artbank** has leased art since 1980: ~11,000-work collection, individual work rentals **$165–$11,000/year**, minimum spend **$550/year**, works refreshable every 12 months, 100% tax-deductible for business; it leased **4,360 artworks to 390 clients in 2021–22** ([Artbank](https://www.artbank.gov.au/art-leasing); [ANAO audit](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/acquisition-management-and-leasing-artworks-artbank); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artbank)). Private players (e.g. [Redrock Artbank](https://www.redrockartbank.com/)) also operate. Rental is a proven model in Australia — but skewed corporate; the consumer/emerging-artist rental niche is largely unserved.

## 2. Artist supply side

**How many artists.** *Artists as Workers* (Throsby & Petetskaya, Macquarie University for Creative Australia, released May 2024; surveys FY2021–22 — the 7th study in the four-decade series) estimates **47,100 practising professional artists** in Australia (plausible range 42,000–53,000), down slightly from **48,000 in the 2016-based *Making Art Work* study (2017 — older data)** ([Creative Australia](https://creative.gov.au/research/artists-workers-economic-study-professional-artists-australia); [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-05-07/creative-australia-artists-at-work-artist-income-inequality/103803622); [*Making Art Work*](https://creative.gov.au/research/making-art-work-economic-study-professional-artists-australia)). The study covers eight practice areas including visual artists and craft practitioners ([APO](https://apo.org.au/node/326678)). *Author estimate: applying Queensland's ~20% population share pro rata implies roughly 9,000–9,500 professional artists in Queensland — a derived figure, not from the report.*

**Artist incomes (the core supply-side pain point).** From *Artists as Workers* ([Creative Australia](https://creative.gov.au/news-events/news/major-report-shows-economic-conditions-artists-odds-value-we-place-arts); [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-05-07/creative-australia-artists-at-work-artist-income-inequality/103803622); [ArtsHub](https://www.artshub.com.au/news/features/whats-changed-for-artists-incomes-throsby-led-research-paints-harsh-view-2723192/)):

- Average income from **creative work alone: $23,200/year** — essentially unchanged in real terms since the mid-1980s.
- Average **total income $54,500** — 26% below the national workforce average; artists earn ~$840/week less than average workers.
- **Half of artists earn under $10,000/year** from creative practice; only **21%** can meet minimum income needs from creative work; **43%** can't meet needs from all income sources combined.
- Only **10%** work exclusively on their creative practice; **80%** are freelance/self-employed; gender pay gap **19%**; **74%** hold university degrees (vs 36% of the general workforce); women are 65% of the artistic workforce.

**Visual/craft artists specifically (newest data).** The four-year ARC Linkage study led by RMIT and the University of Melbourne (*Visual Arts Work*, published April 2025) found average income **from visual art/craft practice of just $13,937 in 2023–24**; men averaged $23,130 vs women $12,330 (a 47% practice-income gender gap); ~50% did unpaid arts work; 63% reported stress about paying for essentials; the sector was likened to the gig economy ([RMIT News](https://www.rmit.edu.au/news/all-news/2025/apr/arts-workers); survey background: [AAANZ](https://aaanz.info/survey-visual-arts-work-national-survey-of-visual-artists-craft-artists-and-arts-workers/)).

**Pipeline of new artists (dated — flag).** In 2016 there were **17,017 bachelor completions in Creative Arts**, with completions growing 92% between 2001 and 2016 (vs 39% for all degrees), and ~140,000 creative arts graduates in the labour market; 62% female ([Australia Council Artfacts](http://artfacts.australiacouncil.gov.au/overview/creation-11/ov-fact12/); [Curtin/AJLE analysis](https://www.curtin.edu.au/resources/file/faculty/fbl/127098-AJLE-Vol-23-No-1-2020-Text_article-2-final.pdf)). Even if only a fraction pursue visual arts practice, thousands of new emerging artists enter the market each year — most without gallery representation.

**Where emerging artists currently sell.** With dealer representation shrinking ([ArtsHub](https://www.artshub.com.au/news/opinions-analysis/the-changing-landscape-of-artist-dealer-representation-2764668/)), emerging artists rely on a patchwork: online marketplaces (Bluethumb's 20,000 artists — [Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/life/collectibles/digital-marketplace-bluethumb-paints-australian-artists-a-50-million-picture/)); Instagram/social selling; design markets such as **Finders Keepers** (Brisbane roughly twice yearly, curated emerging designers/artists — [Finders Keepers](https://www.thefinderskeepers.com/blog/tag/brisbane-markets/); [Concrete Playground](https://concreteplayground.com/brisbane/event/the-finders-keepers-pop-up-market)); artist-run initiatives (ARIs); art prizes (e.g. the churchie, Australia's longest-running emerging art prize — [BNE ART](https://bneart.com/)); and open-studio events. None of these offer recurring income; a rental model would be a genuine novelty for this cohort.

## 3. Consumer demand side

**Participation is at record highs.** The **2025 National Arts Participation Survey** (*Creative Transformations*, Creative Australia, released 3 June 2026; n=9,065 Australians 15+ — the latest edition):

- **98%** of Australians engage with the arts; **74% attended live arts events (15.4 million people)** — the highest since the series began in 2009; **43% (9 million)** actively create or collaborate in art-making ([Creative Australia](https://creative.gov.au/news-events/news/more-australians-attending-arts-ever-cost-growing-barrier); [report page](https://creative.gov.au/research/creative-transformations-results-national-arts-participation-survey)).
- **Cost is the biggest barrier: 60%** cite it (up from 55% in 2022) and **55% missed events they wanted to attend due to cost**.
- One in three attended First Nations arts events; among purchasers of First Nations art/products, **authenticity (79%), fair payment to artists (77%) and quality (77%)** dominate purchase criteria — evidence that Australian buyers care about provenance and fair artist pay, both core to AccessArt's pitch.
- In the prior (2022) survey, 40% of Victorians attended visual arts and craft events ([Creative Victoria](https://creative.vic.gov.au/resources/audience-participation-survey)) — visual arts is a mainstream, not niche, interest.

**Who buys art (global data — flag as not Australia-specific).** UBS/Art Basel and Arts Economics surveys of collectors show **millennials and Gen X are now the biggest-spending cohorts**, and **women collectors outspend men** (34% of women vs 25% of men spent over US$1m across two years) ([Artnet](https://news.artnet.com/market/art-wealth-survey-women-millennials-1309265); [Art Basel](https://www.artbasel.com/news/millennials-art-market-2020?lang=en)). Millennials discover art primarily via Instagram/social media (44% of 18–24s; 34% of 25–34s) ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20160413005960/en/Study-U.S.-Millennials-Prefer-Instagram-to-Museums-Purchase-Discover-Art-Online)). Bluethumb's Australian buyer base spans "first-time art buyers to established collectors and architecture firms" ([Bluethumb](https://bluethumb.com.au/)); no public Australian age/income breakdown of art buyers exists — a gap AccessArt should fill with its own survey.

**Online purchase willingness.** COVID broke the online-art-buying taboo in Australia — Bluethumb "sales records were broken" as resistant buyers were forced to try it, and the business "roughly doubled every year" ([Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/news/entrepreneurs/bluethumb-online-gallery-turning-starving-artists-into-millionaires/)). Globally, the Hiscox Online Art Trade Report series (final edition 2023) tracked a maturing online trade with younger collectors most open to new models (e.g. 78% of younger collectors likely to try fractional ownership) ([Hiscox](https://www.hiscox.co.uk/online-art-trade-report); [ArtTactic](https://arttactic.com/reports/online-art-market-report-2024)).

**The mass-market substitute.** The realistic competitor for a first artwork is not a gallery but Kmart/IKEA wall décor: Kmart has become "Australia's affordable style giant", deliberately targeting design-conscious younger consumers at famously low prices ([The Times AU](https://www.thetimes.com.au/house-garden/50583-kmarts-homewares-revolution-how-a-discount-retailer-became-australias-affordable-style-giant); [Kmart wall art](https://www.kmart.com.au/category/home-and-living/wall-art/); [IKEA wall art](https://www.ikea.com/au/en/cat/wall-art-10788/)). The trade-off consumers accept is ubiquity — "mass-market designs may be widely seen in other homes" ([House of Isabella](https://houseofisabella.com.au/pages/kmart-canvas-prints)). AccessArt's rental price point can undercut the *psychological* cost of original art while beating Kmart on uniqueness and story.

## 4. South East Queensland specifics

**Population and growth.** SEQ is Australia's fastest-growing region: KPMG analysis (Dec 2025) projects **4.5 million people by the 2032 Brisbane Olympics** and **~6 million by 2046**, with growth of ~2.2%/year over the past five years vs 1.5% nationally; the last five years added ~68,000 on the Gold Coast, 61,000 in Logan–Beaudesert, 51,600 in Ipswich and 47,500 on the Sunshine Coast ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/au/en/media/media-releases/2025/12/south-east-queensland-population-soars.html); [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-01/south-east-queensland-population-growth-2032-olympics/106085290)). Greater Brisbane's estimated resident population was **~2.78 million at June 2024**, growing 2%+ a year — among the fastest of any capital ([ABS Regional Population](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/population/regional-population/latest-release)). The Gold Coast is forecast to pass **1 million by 2046** and the Sunshine Coast to exceed **540,000** ([ABC/QGSO projections](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-01/south-east-queensland-population-growth-2032-olympics/106085290); [Sunshine Coast Council](https://www.sunshinecoast.qld.gov.au/experience-sunshine-coast/statistics-and-maps/population-forecast)). The **2032 Olympics** is driving a decade of infrastructure investment and cultural programming across the corridor ([Qld Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/about/brisbane2032)).

The **~35% renter share** matters strategically: renters move often, are wary of investing in permanent décor, and often can't hang heavy works — art *rental/exchange* fits their life stage better than purchase.

**Brisbane arts scene.** Brisbane has a dense, emerging-artist-oriented ecosystem: **Brisbane Art Design (BAD)** — Museum of Brisbane's annual May festival of open studios, workshops and neighbourhood activations showcasing local artists and designers ([Museum of Brisbane](https://www.museumofbrisbane.com.au/about-museum-of-brisbane/programs-initiatives/brisbane-art-design/)); an active ARI scene (e.g. House Conspiracy in West End) documented by the **BNE ART Brisbane Art Guide** ([bneart.com](https://bneart.com/); [ARI archive](https://bneart.com/tag/artist-run-initiative/)); **the churchie emerging art prize**, Australia's longest-running early-career art prize ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_churchie_emerging_art_prize)); and curated design markets — **Finders Keepers** runs Brisbane markets roughly twice a year connecting shoppers with emerging artists/designers, and has previously run a Finders Keepers × BAD pop-up ([Finders Keepers](https://www.thefinderskeepers.com/blog/tag/brisbane-markets/); [Concrete Playground](https://concreteplayground.com/brisbane/event/the-finders-keepers-x-bad-art-design-pop-up-market)). These are natural artist-acquisition and pop-up channels for AccessArt.

## 5. Market sizing inputs (TAM/SAM/SOM building blocks)

Concrete, citable inputs for a financial model (derived figures flagged):

- **National TAM anchors:** ~10.85m Australian households (2021 Census-based; derive current from ABS); household visual arts/craft spend $1,042m/yr at 2009–10 ([ABS 4172.0](https://www.abs.gov.au/ausstats/abs@.nsf/Lookup/4172.0main+features212014) — *dated; CPI-uplift with a flag*); galleries/museums industry $2.5–2.6bn ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/au/market-size/art-galleries-museums/)); auction market ~$136–150m/yr 2023–25 ([ArtVals](https://www.artvals.com/news/global-art-market-trends-2026)).
- **SAM (SEQ):** SEQ population ~4.0–4.2m now → 4.5m by 2032 ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/au/en/media/media-releases/2025/12/south-east-queensland-population-soars.html)); *derived:* at 2.6 persons/household ([ABS](https://abs.gov.au/census/find-census-data/quickstats/2021/3GBRI)) that is **~1.55–1.6m SEQ households today, ~1.75m by 2032** (author calculation). Greater Brisbane alone: 1.02m dwellings (2021), median household income ~$96k/yr, 35.1% renting.
- **Propensity proxies:** 74% attend arts events; 43% actively participate; 60% blocked by cost ([NAPS 2025](https://creative.gov.au/news-events/news/more-australians-attending-arts-ever-cost-growing-barrier)). *No Australian "share of households buying original art annually" statistic exists — the last ABS purchase survey dates to 1997 ([ABS 4172.0 2004](https://abs.gov.au/ausstats/abs@.nsf/bb8db737e2af84b8ca2571780015701e/259252694AC748F9CA2571E0000618EB?opendocument=)). Model a 2–5% annual buying-household assumption range and validate with primary research.*
- **Price/ARPU benchmarks:** Artbank rentals $165–$11,000/work/yr, $550/yr minimum ([Artbank](https://www.artbank.gov.au/art-leasing)); Bluethumb cart size tripled in a decade, 110,000 works sold nationally ([Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/life/collectibles/digital-marketplace-bluethumb-paints-australian-artists-a-50-million-picture/)).
- **Supply:** 47,100 professional artists nationally ([Creative Australia](https://creative.gov.au/research/artists-workers-economic-study-professional-artists-australia)); *derived* ~9,000+ in Queensland (author pro-rata estimate); plus thousands of annual creative-arts graduates (17,017 completions in 2016 — [Artfacts](http://artfacts.australiacouncil.gov.au/overview/creation-11/ov-fact12/), *dated*). Artist income desperation (half under $10k/yr creative income) implies very low supply-side acquisition cost.

## 6. Key takeaways for AccessArt

- **Demand and supply are both strong but disconnected.** Record 74% arts attendance and 9m creative participants sit alongside artists earning $23,200 (all artforms) / $13,937 (visual & craft) from practice — a marketplace that closes this gap has a clear two-sided value proposition.
- **Cost is the #1 barrier (60%) — rental is the wedge.** A $30–80/month rental tier (benchmarked against Artbank's $550/yr minimum) converts the Kmart-print buyer into an original-art customer without the purchase-commitment barrier.
- **The gallery channel is shrinking, not the market.** 2024–25 gallery closures mean more unrepresented emerging artists seeking channels — artist acquisition should be cheap and fast, especially via BAD, Finders Keepers, ARIs and the churchie ecosystem in Brisbane.
- **Online original-art buying is proven in Australia.** Bluethumb's 110,000 sales and 20,000 artists de-risk the channel; AccessArt differentiates on rental/exchange + local (SEQ) discovery, not on inventing online art buying.
- **SEQ timing is exceptional.** Fastest-growing region in the country, 4.5m people by the 2032 Olympics, a decade of civic/cultural investment, and a young (median age 36), 35%-renter Brisbane population that suits rental economics.
- **Provenance and fair artist pay sell.** 77–79% of First Nations art buyers rank authenticity and fair payment as top criteria — build artist stories, transparent revenue splits and (for First Nations work) Indigenous Art Code compliance into the product from day one.
- **Data gap = research opportunity.** There is no current Australian statistic on the share of households buying original art or their demographics; a 300–500-person SEQ survey would both fill the model's key assumption (buying propensity) and generate launch PR.
- **Flag the stale data in any investor deck.** ABS household art spend (2009–10), graduate numbers (2016) and Throsby's *Making Art Work* (2017) are old; lead with *Artists as Workers* (2024), RMIT *Visual Arts Work* (2025) and NAPS 2025 (released June 2026).
