# Competitor Research: Online Art Marketplaces & Rental Platforms

**Research date:** 30 August 2026 · **Prepared for:** AccessArt (rent / buy / exchange original art direct from emerging artists, launching in South East Queensland)

## Executive summary

The Australian online art market is dominated by one player — Bluethumb — which raised its commission to 35% + GST (38.5% all-in) in May 2025, continuing a decade-long upward creep that has generated visible artist resentment; its Gold Coast-based rival Art Lovers Australia charges 33% plus onboarding fees. Every significant sales platform, local and international, raised fees in 2025–26 (Saatchi Art to 40%, Artfinder to 40–45% plus subscription), creating a clear pricing wedge for a lower-take challenger. Crucially, **no Australian platform offers consumer art rental as a marketplace product**: rental in Australia is either the government's Artbank program ($165–$11,000/yr per work, minimum $550/yr spend) or small corporate-focused gallery businesses (Moving Canvas, Rental Art Australia, Artfirst — two of them SEQ-based legacy operators). The only proven consumer art-rental marketplaces globally are Rise Art (UK, ~5–10% of artwork value per month with rent-credit-to-buy) and Curina (NYC, $38–$348/month tiers with 100% rental credit toward purchase) — both alive, both modestly funded, and both useful pricing templates. The graveyard (TWYLA, Paddle8, Vango, Auctionata, Art Money's 2024 halt) shows art startups die from demand-side failure and capital-hungry models, not from lack of artist supply — supply is abundant and increasingly frustrated with Instagram's collapsed organic reach.

## 1. Australian platforms

### 1.1 Bluethumb — the dominant incumbent

- **Model:** Open online marketplace for original Australian art (web + app). Founded 2012 in Adelaide by Edward and George Hartley. Sales only — no consumer rental product (it has a "rental"-tagged artwork search page but no rental service).
- **Commission/fees:** On **9 May 2025 commission rose from 30% + GST (33%) to 35% + GST (38.5% all-in)**, applying to all artists on free and paid plans ([Bluethumb Help Centre](https://docs.bluethumb.com.au/en/articles/11058300-commission-changes-2025)). Historical creep: ~20% at launch → 25% → 30% + GST → 35% + GST ([Solene Haus analysis](https://www.solenehaus.com/blog/how-much-does-bluethumb-take-from-artists)). Optional **Bluethumb Pro** subscription is $29/month (annual = 2 months free) for scheduling/exclusivity/marketing features — it does **not** reduce commission ([Pro Plan FAQ](https://docs.bluethumb.com.au/en/articles/3007330-bluethumb-pro-plan-overview-faqs)). A lower 20% rate for Indigenous artists/art centres was reported at the 2023 US launch ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/bluethumb--australias-largest-online-art-marketplace--expands-to-the-us-creating-a-one-of-a-kind-platform-for-original-diverse-artists-stateside-301951549.html)) — *not re-verified after the 2025 increase*.
- **Onboarding/vetting:** Effectively open — any Australian artist can join free and upload; artists set prices (must include commission). No curatorial gate.
- **Scale:** 20,000+ artists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluethumb_Art_Gallery)); some 2025 sources cite 30,000+ (*discrepancy — treat 20k+ as the safe floor*). 110,000+ originals sold; passed **A$50 million cumulative artist revenue in 2024** ([Forbes Australia](https://www.forbes.com.au/life/collectibles/digital-marketplace-bluethumb-paints-australian-artists-a-50-million-picture/), [Bluethumb blog](https://bluethumb.com.au/blog/news-and-media/bluethumbs-journey-to-50-million-in-artist-revenue/)).
- **Funding:** Modest — ~$1.16M Series A (QUT Creative Enterprise Australia, Grand Prix Capital, Equity Venture Partners) per [Crunchbase](https://www.crunchbase.com/organization/bluethumb); largely revenue-funded since.
- **Status 2025–26:** Active and growing; expanded to the US in October 2023 (bluethumbart.com); its "[2025 Wrapped](https://bluethumb.com.au/blog/bluethumb-team/bluethumb-art-2025-wrapped/)" post claims thousands of new artists and record sales (exact figures published only as images).
- **Read for AccessArt:** Bluethumb proves the open-supply, artist-first marketplace works in Australia — but its rising take rate reflects the real cost of demand generation, and artist blogs now openly ask whether it's "worth it" ([Solene Haus](https://www.solenehaus.com/guides/is-bluethumb-worth-it-for-australian-artists)).

### 1.2 Art Lovers Australia — the SEQ incumbent

- **Model:** Online gallery + physical Gold Coast gallery (Southport, opened 2020). Founded 2016. Sales, commissions of custom work, workshops.
- **Fees:** **30% + GST (33%) commission** on all sales per its [terms of service](https://artloversaustralia.com.au/terms-of-service-2-2/); third-party review reports an additional **~$90 setup fee and $10/month** platform fee ([Solene Haus](https://www.solenehaus.com/blog/is-art-lovers-australia-worth-it-for-artists)) — *the recurring-fee figures come from a reviewer, not ALA's own pages; verify before quoting*. Promotional discounts (10–18%) are shared between platform and artist. Prices must build in packing/shipping.
- **Scale/status:** 1,000+ artists, ~50,000 works listed ([Art Guide](https://artguide.com.au/gallery/art-lovers-australia-gallery/), [ALA](https://artloversaustralia.com.au/)); active 2026. Light-touch curation ("curated pieces" claimed, but onboarding is application-based rather than exclusive).
- **Read:** This is AccessArt's closest geographic competitor (Southport, QLD) — sales only, no rental, fee-stack heavier than Bluethumb's for low-volume artists.

### 1.3 Artbank — government art leasing (the rental benchmark)

- **Model:** Australian Government program (est. 1980) that **buys works outright from living Australian artists** and leases its ~11,000-work collection to businesses and private homes.
- **Pricing:** Individual works lease for **$165–$11,000/year; minimum annual spend $550**; tax-deductible for business lessees ([artbank.gov.au](https://www.artbank.gov.au/art-leasing), [home leasing](https://www.artbank.gov.au/art-leasing-your-home-or-private-space)).
- **Status:** Operating (45th anniversary celebrated 2025 — [ArtsHub](https://www.artshub.com.au/news/reviews/artbank-on-display-review-45-years-worth-celebrating-2817598/)).
- **Read:** Artbank validates art rental in Australia and sets the consumer's mental price anchor (~$3.20+/week at the floor), but it supports artists via one-off acquisition, skews corporate, curates heavily, and offers no marketplace dynamics. A $550/yr minimum leaves the casual consumer under-served.

### 1.4 SEQ / regional rental businesses (legacy, non-marketplace)

- **Moving Canvas** — Brisbane art-rental gallery **since 2004**, now physically in Caloundra (Sunshine Coast), serving Brisbane and interstate via courier; corporate + residential; minimum 1-month rental then week-by-week, no lock-in; also sells and offers curatorial consulting ([movingcanvas.com.au](https://movingcanvas.com.au/)). Active.
- **Rental Art Australia** — family-owned Queensland business **since 1989**; 1,000+ pieces (originals, limited editions, reproductions); advertises rental **from ~$2–3/week**, tax-deductible, corporate-leaning ([rentalart.com.au](https://www.rentalart.com.au/)). Active.
- **Others:** Artfirst Australia (corporate rental, "lowest rates" positioning, [artfirst.com.au](https://artfirst.com.au/)); Artist BANX (Sunshine Coast rental + sales, [banx.com.au](https://banx.com.au/)); Wentworth Galleries (Sydney, 6–36-month rentals with purchase option); Studio Gallery Group (1-day to 36-month leases).
- **Read:** Rental supply and demand exist in SEQ — but it's all gallery-consignment, staging/corporate-focused, offline-first, and none is a two-sided marketplace or an emerging-artist brand.

### 1.5 Art Money — BNPL for art (cautionary tale, partially recovered)

- **Model:** Sydney-founded fintech; interest-free 10-month instalments for art purchases via ~2,000 partner galleries; backed by Christie's Ventures and 135 investors.
- **What happened:** **Halted all new lending on 10 June 2024** after running out of growth capital — CEO Paul Becker said a further $5M was needed to reach profitability by end-2025; causes cited were the art-market contraction, dried-up equity markets, and interest-rate margin squeeze ([Electronic Payments International](https://www.electronicpaymentsinternational.com/news/bnpl-outfit-art-money-halts-operations/), [ArtsHub](https://www.artshub.co.uk/news/news/art-money-pulls-back-in-economic-squeeze-2616370/)).
- **Status Aug 2026:** [artmoney.com](https://artmoney.com/) is live and marketing Interest Free (£1,000–£100,000 purchases), a "Verified Buyer" AML/KYC product, and a Buyer's Premium product "coming soon to the UK" — it appears to have resumed in at least some markets, but **I could not verify when/whether full lending resumed or its Australian availability; treat current lending status as unconfirmed**.
- **Read:** capital-intensive art models are fragile — a strong argument for consignment-based rental (artist keeps ownership) rather than inventory- or receivables-heavy models.

### 1.6 Art St (artst.com.au) — it survived, barely changed

- The Brisbane subscription gallery flagged from 2019 is **still online in 2025–26** (site copyright "© Art St 2013–2025"). Current artist pricing per its [artist info page](https://www.artst.com.au/artist-info.html): **$400/year subscription + $30 per artwork listing fee, 0% commission on general sales**, and 15% commission only on works created through its "Art Request" commissioning feature. (2019 pricing was reportedly $420/yr incl. 3 listings — so essentially static.)
- **Read:** It survived by staying tiny — a local directory-style gallery with painting groups, not a venture-scale competitor. *Sales volume and real activity level unverifiable from public sources.* Its persistence suggests a subscription-not-commission model can cover costs at micro scale, but shows no evidence it can scale demand.

### 1.7 Art Pharmacy — pivoted away from consumers

- Founded in Sydney by Emilya Colliver as an online gallery selling affordable emerging Australian art (and historically associated with art subscription/rental offerings). As of 2026 it presents purely as a **B2B art & culture consultancy** — public art, placemaking, corporate collections ([artpharmacy.com.au](https://artpharmacy.com.au/about/)). *No consumer marketplace or subscription product is visible on the current site; I could not verify the details of the discontinued consumer offering.*
- **Read:** an Australian precedent of consumer art subscription economics not holding, with the pivot going to corporate/government money.

### 1.8 Newer Australian entrants

- **Makeshift** ([makeshift-au.com](https://makeshift-au.com/)) — Melbourne-based, community-led curated marketplace for independent Australian artists/creators. Small; scale and fees not publicly documented.
- **StateoftheART Australia** — online gallery promoting emerging and established Australian artists (mentioned in [GoDaddy's AU guide](https://www.godaddy.com/resources/au/smallbusiness/where-to-sell-art-online)); small.
- No funded, venture-scale new entrant in Australian consumer art marketplaces was found for 2024–2026 — the field is Bluethumb, ALA, and long-tail boutiques.

## 2. International reference players

### Sales marketplaces

- **Saatchi Art (US)** — world's largest open artist marketplace. Free to list, effectively no vetting. Commission **raised from 35% to 40%** (artists keep 60%); sources date the increase to 2025–26 and report artists were poorly notified ([Saatchi help centre](https://support.saatchiart.com/hc/en-us/articles/14567125883547-Commission-Rate), [ZicoArt comparison](https://www.zicoarts.com/what-percentage-do-art-platforms-take/), [artist commentary](https://www.raullara.net/my-experience-as-an-artist-on-saatchi-art-what-to-know-before-selling-on-the-platform/)). *Exact effective date not verified.* Buyer promo discounts (10–20%) come off the price before the split. No monthly fee.
- **Artfinder (UK)** — **curated** (application review). Subscription + commission: **Core $14/mo at 45% commission; Premium $40/mo at 40%** (commission on artwork value, not shipping); plans revamped late 2025 with managed shipping on both tiers and a new annual loyalty bonus of 2–7% for artists earning >$26,500/yr ([Artfinder seller support](https://sellers.artfinder.com/article/781-what-are-the-different-seller-plan-subscriptions), [2026 guide](https://www.raullara.net/guide-to-sell-art-on-artfinder/)).
- **Singulart (France)** — heavily curated, invitation/application only; commission reported at **50%** as of 2025 (older sources cite 30–50% by artist renown) ([review](https://wastedtalentinc.com/singulart/), [Artlisti](https://artlisti.com/reviews/singulart-review/)). Large VC funding (reported ~€60M raised in 2021 — *not re-verified*). High-touch: art advisors, logistics handled.
- **Artsy (US)** — B2B: galleries (not individual artists) pay a **monthly subscription (~US$450/mo reported) plus 5–19% e-commerce commission** by tier ([Budget Collector comparison](https://www.budgetcollector.org/budget-collector-vs-artsy-compare-pricing-product-features-and-more/), [Art World Marketing guide](https://artworldmarketing.com/guides/artsy-and-artnet-for-art-galleries/)). *Fee figures are third-party; Artsy doesn't publish pricing.* Irrelevant to emerging unrepresented artists except as an aspiration.
- **Etsy (as art channel)** — open, zero vetting, colossal traffic. Fee stack 2026: **$0.20 listing (4 months) + 6.5% transaction on item + shipping + payment processing (US: 3% + $0.25) + mandatory 12% Offsite Ads fee for sellers over $10k/yr (15% optional below); ~12–20% effective total** ([Craftybase](https://craftybase.com/blog/the-complete-guide-to-etsy-fees), [Printify](https://printify.com/blog/how-much-does-etsy-take-per-sale/)). Cheap, but original art drowns among prints, mass goods and AI-generated imagery — discoverability for one-off originals is poor.

### Rental players (the models that matter most to AccessArt)

- **Rise Art (UK)** — curated marketplace doing **both sales and consumer rental** since 2010. Rental pricing: **roughly 5–10% of the artwork's retail value per month** (worked example: £2,600 painting rents at £183/month); billed monthly, cancel anytime; optional damage waiver at checkout; works arrive ready to hang. **Rent-to-buy credit: 100% of the first month + 50% of every later month credits toward purchase** ([riseart.com/about/rentals](https://www.riseart.com/about/rentals), [customer help](https://help.riseart.com/article/11-how-does-renting-art-work)). Corporate arm (Rise Art Pro) rents to offices. **Still operating** — UK accounts filed to Sept 2025, next due Sept 2026 ([Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/07099261)); total funding only ~US$2.1M over 4 rounds ([Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/rise-art/__LuWVNgyCoWRBnu_9T25N0fHmyCc4oNn1_3j-ZXnQHDU)) — a 15-year-old lean survivor, not a rocket.
- **Curina (US, NYC)** — founded 2019 (Columbia University startup); rents original works from **local living artists** on flat monthly tiers — historically **$38 / $88 / $148 per month** by size/price band, with the live site now also showing $248 and $348 tiers; **minimum 3 months**, then continue, swap, return, or buy with **100% of rental payments credited to the purchase price** ([curina.co](https://www.curina.co/), [Bushwick Daily](https://bushwickdaily.com/arts-culture/nyc-curina-rent-art/)). Still operating in 2026; NYC-centric with US shipping. Artist split not published.
- **TWYLA (US, Austin)** — **dead**. Raised **$14M led by Google Ventures (Oct 2016)** to sell exclusive limited-edition prints ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/10/06/twyla-which-sells-exclusive-artists-prints-has-just-raised-14-million-led-by-gv/)); suffered a sexist-ad PR debacle, a fraud lawsuit against its founding CEO, leadership churn (HomeAway's Brian Sharples stepped in, 2017 — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/09/08/homeaway-cofounder-brian-sharples-takes-the-helm-of-twyla-for-now/)), and quietly closed (Yelp lists it CLOSED). *Exact shutdown date not publicly documented.*

## 3. Informal competition: Instagram, Facebook Marketplace, Etsy-direct

- **Instagram** is emerging artists' default gallery: zero fees on DM-negotiated sales, but organic reach has collapsed — industry reporting says **87% of businesses saw significant reach decline over 18 months**, and a 10k-follower account now reaches ~200–300 people per post vs ~1,000–1,500 in 2020 ([ArtCabbage](https://artcabbage.com/instagram-art-decline/), [analysis](https://www.jasminedirectory.com/blog/instagrams-algorithm-changes-death-blow-to-small-business-reach/)). Artists carry all payment risk (bank transfer/PayPal), shipping logistics, and zero buyer trust infrastructure. Commentary through 2025–26 shows artists shifting to email lists and offline sales ([Execute Magazine](https://executemagazine.com/how-to-survive-the-algorithm-in-2026/), [Street Art Cities](https://streetartcities.substack.com/p/why-instagram-no-longer-works-for)).
- **Facebook Marketplace** is free and hyper-local — relevant for SEQ — but has no art context: buyers expect haggling and pickup, there's no provenance, insurance, or emerging-artist discovery.
- **Etsy** (fees above) is the cheapest formal channel but treats a one-off original like a commodity SKU; visibility effectively requires paying Etsy Ads, and the platform's brand pulls price expectations down.
- **Implication:** the informal channels supply AccessArt's artist-acquisition story: reach is dying, admin is painful, and a 12–20% (Etsy) to 33–40% (marketplaces) fee spread frames what artists will tolerate for genuine demand generation.

## 5. Gap analysis

**Mapping the field on two axes — (a) sales vs rental, (b) open vs curated:**

- *Open + sales:* Bluethumb, Saatchi, Etsy — crowded, and all raised prices in 2025–26.
- *Curated + sales:* Artfinder, Singulart, Art Lovers Australia — differentiated on trust, expensive for artists.
- *Curated + rental:* Artbank, Rise Art, Curina, and SEQ's legacy rental galleries — small set, mostly corporate or single-city.
- *Open + consumer rental + marketplace + Australia:* **empty.** This is AccessArt's cell. No Australian platform lets a consumer rent a $600 painting from a Logan or West End emerging artist through a marketplace, with a path to buy or swap.

**On "no-vetting" positioning:** openness alone is not a differentiator — Bluethumb and Saatchi are already effectively open, and Etsy is entirely open. The curated players use vetting as their *buyer-side* trust signal. The defensible combination is **open artist onboarding + strong buyer-side quality surfacing** (editorial picks, collections, style/room matching), plus the rental/exchange mechanic that nobody local offers. The "exchange" leg is genuinely novel: the closest analogue is Curina's mid-rental swap, and no platform supports artist-to-artist or collector-to-collector exchange.

**Who died, and why:**

- **Paddle8** (US auctions, raised $44M) — Chapter 11 in March 2020; never profitable, funded operations with debt, "prioritised expansion over developing its identity" ([Center for Art Law](https://itsartlaw.org/art-law/a-melting-ice-cube-the-bankruptcy-of-paddle8/), [Artsy](https://www.artsy.net/article/artsy-editorial-online-auction-platform-paddle8-filed-bankruptcy)).
- **Auctionata** (Paddle8's merger partner) — insolvent; couldn't raise fast enough.
- **TWYLA** — $14M from Google Ventures couldn't buy demand for exclusive prints; governance and brand missteps compounded it.
- **Vango** (mobile/AR art marketplace) — quietly disappeared; no post-mortem published.
- **Art Money** — halted June 2024 when the capital-hungry receivables model met higher interest rates and a shrinking art market.
- **Art Pharmacy** — Australian consumer subscription/gallery pivoted to B2B consultancy; consumer art subscription cashflow evidently didn't sustain it.
- **Art St** — survived precisely by *not* scaling: near-zero cost base, subscription revenue, no growth ambition.

Common thread: **artist supply was never the problem — demand generation and burn were.** The survivors (Bluethumb, Rise Art, Curina, Moving Canvas, Rental Art Australia) are lean, revenue-funded, and grew slowly.

## 6. Key takeaways for AccessArt

1. **Price against a rising incumbent.** Bluethumb now takes 38.5% all-in and ALA 33% + fees; Saatchi 40%, Artfinder 40–45% + subscription. A transparent flat commission in the **15–25% band** is a genuinely disruptive recruiting message — but Bluethumb's own fee history is a warning that demand generation is what the commission pays for; model CAC honestly before promising a low take rate forever.
2. **The consumer-rental gap in Australia is real and specific.** Artbank ($550/yr minimum, curated, corporate-skewed) and SEQ's legacy rental galleries (corporate/staging-focused, offline) leave the young renter/home consumer unserved. AccessArt would be first in Australia to do marketplace-model consumer rental from emerging artists.
3. **Copy the proven rental mechanics.** Benchmarks: Rise Art at ~5–10% of artwork value/month with 100% of month 1 + 50% thereafter credited to purchase; Curina at flat $38–$348/month tiers, 3-month minimum, 100% credited. Flat tiers are simpler to market; percentage pricing scales better with artwork value. Rent-to-own credit appears to be table stakes.
4. **Rental logistics are the hard part.** Both rental incumbents bundle ready-to-hang delivery and an optional damage waiver; SEQ launch needs a costed answer for delivery, insurance, returns, and artwork condition management — this, not the website, is the moat and the margin risk.
5. **Stay consignment-based; own nothing.** Art Money (receivables) and Artbank (an acquisition budget only a government sustains) show capital-heavy art models are fragile. Artists retain ownership; AccessArt takes a revenue share on rentals and sales.
6. **Open onboarding needs a buyer-trust layer.** "No vetting" matches Bluethumb/Saatchi, so it wins artists but not buyers. Pair it with curation-as-merchandising (featured collections, local-artist storytelling) to compete with the trust signal that Artfinder/Singulart/Rise Art sell.
7. **Artist acquisition tailwind: Instagram is failing them.** Collapsed organic reach (~2–3% of followers per post) and fee fatigue mean motivated supply; the pitch is "we generate demand and handle payments/logistics for less than the incumbents."
8. **Demand-side discipline decides survival.** Every corpse in this market died of insufficient buyers or excessive burn, never of insufficient art. SEQ-first density (local delivery, local artist stories, staging/interior-design partnerships as an early B2B revenue floor à la Moving Canvas) is the pragmatic path before national expansion.
