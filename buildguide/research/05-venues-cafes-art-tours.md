# Venues, Cafés and Art Tours: Display Models, Trails and the Hospitality Side of AccessArt

**Research date: 30 August 2026.** Sources cited inline; unverifiable claims flagged.

**Executive summary.** The "art on café walls" model is old, widespread and almost entirely informal: artists get space through walk-in asks and Facebook groups, venues take 0–30% commission (often 0%), and both sides suffer the same pain points everywhere — no sales mechanism at point of display, work hanging for months untracked, damage from grease/steam, no insurance, and staff who can't answer "is this for sale?". A handful of organisations have formalised the model — Café Art in London (charity, 80/20 split to artist, largely wound down post-COVID), Artwalls in San Diego (a near-exact AccessArt analog: QR-on-wall → Stripe checkout, artist 60–85%, venue 15%, platform ~2%), TurningArt and ArtLifting in the US corporate market, and Australia's own Artbank plus a cluster of commercial art-rental firms — but nobody has combined QR-driven café sales with council-style art trails in one platform, and no such platform exists in South East Queensland. SEQ meanwhile has proven appetite for exactly this trail format: the Council of Mayors' SEQ Food Trails platform (13 councils), Brisbane's BAD festival (150+ events across 25+ venues), Brisbane Open House, and the Granite Belt Art & Craft Trail all show that mapped, self-guided venue trails get funded and used. The main structural risk is venue churn — Australian cafés/restaurants are currently failing at roughly double the national business failure rate — which argues for a venue deal that costs venues nothing and pays them a cut, and for building density in a few walkable precincts before spreading.

## 1. Existing café/venue art programs and platforms

### Café Art (London) — the charity/café model
Café Art, founded 2012, framed and hung artwork by people affected by homelessness (created in art sessions run by London homeless-sector organisations) in independent London cafés, and sold it off the walls: **80% of proceeds to the artist, 20% back into the project** ([Café Art](https://www.cafeart.org.uk/), [Cafe Reality interview](https://www.cafereality.co.uk/blog/cafe-art-where-homelessness-great-art-coffee-meet)). The charity handled framing, hanging and rotation across cafés, working with twelve partner organisations and sister projects in New York and Melbourne. Crucially for AccessArt's planning: **the café display arm ran 2012–2020 and was largely discontinued when COVID closed many partner cafés** — a demonstration of the venue-churn risk at the heart of this model ([Café Art / MyWorld Creative Projects](https://www.myworldcreativeprojects.org/art-in-cafes)). Its calendar (MyLondon photography project) survived as the stronger revenue line.

### Artwalls (San Diego, US) — the closest AccessArt analog
[Artwalls](https://artwalls.space/) ("Art on Every Wall") is a young marketplace doing almost exactly the AccessArt model: venues list wall space (dimensions, photos, aesthetic); artists apply, get approved and hang their own work; customers **scan a QR code next to the artwork and buy via Stripe checkout**. The split on a $100 sale: **artist $60–85, venue $15, platform fee 2% or less on paid plans; free for venues, always**. It launched in San Diego and publishes nothing on rotation schedules, insurance or dispute handling — the gaps AccessArt can differentiate on. Its existence validates the mechanics but also confirms nobody has this locked up globally, let alone in Australia.

### TurningArt (US) — office/venue art subscription at scale
[TurningArt](https://www.turningart.com/how-it-works) is the mature US corporate version: an art advisor curates for the client's space, and the company handles "everything from design and curation to installation and rotation," combining rental rotation with purchase/commission of anchor pieces. Artist economics ([Partner Artist FAQ](https://www.turningart.com/partner-artist-faq)): **60% of retail to the artist on original sales; 25% of retail for a one-year print rental, paid monthly; negotiated fees for commissions**. It works with 2,500+ artists and a 50,000+ piece catalogue. The client (business) pays; the artist is paid by the platform. This is the template for AccessArt's office/commercial side (Section 6).

### ArtLifting (US) — social-enterprise placement
[ArtLifting](https://www.artlifting.com/) (Boston, public benefit corporation) sells and licenses work by artists with disabilities or housing insecurity, largely to corporate buyers. Split: **55% to the artist, 1% to an art-supplies/community fund, 44% retained** — pitched against the gallery-standard 50/50 ([Medium profile](https://medium.com/exponential-creativity/artlifting-empowers-homeless-and-disabled-artists-with-online-marketplace-96b959e441ad), [WGBH](https://www.wgbh.org/news/local/2016-07-11/artlifting-a-platform-for-homeless-artists)). Its pivot toward corporate partnerships (licensing, bulk placements) rather than one-off consumer sales is instructive: recurring B2B revenue funded the mission.

### Art Pharmacy (Sydney) and Curatorial+Co. (Sydney) — the AU consultancy model
[Art Pharmacy](https://artpharmacy.com.au/about/) began as an online gallery/pop-up platform selling emerging Australian artists at accessible prices and evolved into a **full-service art consultancy** delivering commissions and activations in "lobbies, corporate offices, hospitality venues, hotels and public infrastructure" ([expertise page](https://artpharmacy.com.au/expertise/)) — e.g. a digital art commission for The Grounds Cafe in a Hunter St lobby ([project](https://artpharmacy.com.au/projects/digital-art-activation-in-sydney-lobby/)). [Curatorial+Co.](https://curatorialandco.com/about/) similarly pairs a gallery/online program of emerging-to-established Australian artists with consultancy for "collectors, architects, interior designers, developers and hospitality groups." Neither publishes commission rates (standard commercial-gallery consignment applies; AU commercial galleries typically take 40–50%). **The pattern: Australian players in this space drifted upmarket to curated B2B commissions — leaving the high-volume, low-price café wall segment unserved.**

### Artsted (Europe/global) — emerging-artist marketplace, no physical venues
[Artsted](https://blog.artsted.com/post/what-is-artsted) is an online marketplace for emerging artists with price-analytics for collectors; **35% commission, free for artists to join** ([founder interview](https://art-verge.com/buy-emerging-art-online-artsted-founder-maryna-rybakova-talks-about-her-new-art-platform/)). Relevant as a pricing/positioning comparator for emerging-artist commission tolerance, not as a venue model — it has no physical placement arm.

### Artbank (Australia) — government art leasing since 1980
[Artbank](https://www.artbank.gov.au/art-leasing) buys contemporary Australian art on the primary market (supporting living artists at acquisition, not via ongoing royalty) and leases it to businesses, government and individuals: **$165–$11,000 per artwork per year, $550 minimum annual spend, tax-deductible for business** ([leasing page](https://www.artbank.gov.au/art-leasing-business-0), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artbank)). 11,000+ works. Artbank normalised "renting rotating Australian art for your office" — AccessArt's commercial pitch rides on a concept businesses already understand.

### Brisbane / SEQ local examples
No platform operates locally; the practice is ad hoc but real. Java Lounge (Paddington) hangs works by local artists above its tables and Scrumptious Reads (Red Hill) runs a downstairs exhibition space with rotating local displays ([Paddington Art Trail write-up](https://eatdrinkandbekerry.net/index.php/2021/07/13/what-you-must-see-on-the-paddington-art-trail/)); Bean Cafe (181 George St) keeps an "ever-changing wall of artwork." Brisbane City Council runs a [Creative Register](https://www.brisbane.qld.gov.au/events-arts-and-culture/art-and-culture/street-art-and-murals) for artists seeking opportunities and an Outdoor Gallery program, but nothing brokering café walls.

## 2. The informal arrangement today — and its pain points

How it actually works now: artists approach cafés directly with a portfolio, respond to noticeboard/Facebook/Instagram callouts, or get a slot via word of mouth. Some venues run open calls (e.g. [Cafe What?'s standing "call to artists"](https://cafewhat.com/call-to-artists-exhibit-your-work-at-cafe-what/) — portfolio by email/WhatsApp/walk-in). Terms are rarely written down.

**Commission norms.** Artist-forum evidence puts café/restaurant commission at **0–30%**, far below gallery rates: a [WetCanvas thread](https://www.wetcanvas.com/forums/topic/commision-on-art-in-local-shop/) reports non-traditional venues (cafés, hairdressers, restaurants) at 0–20%, with French cafés 0–30%; UK council café policies show fixed rates of 10% ([Witney Town Council local art display policy](https://democracy.witney-tc.gov.uk/documents/s18462/1863%20Local%20Art%20Display%20Policy%20For%20Approval.pdf)) up to 35% at one Coach House Café ([Penzance TC report](https://www.penzance-tc.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Arts-and-Culture-30.03.26-Item-6-Reports-for-Decision.pdf)). Many venues take nothing — the art is free décor. For AU context, NAVA's Code of Practice notes even publicly funded galleries' consignment rates vary 10–40% and stresses agreeing rates in writing ([NAVA Code](https://code.visualarts.net.au/selling-and-marketing/public-galleries-selling-work/detailed-discussion)).

**Pain points (artist side)** — a long comment thread on [RedDotBlog's "Showing Your Art in Cafés, Restaurants, Banks and Other Venues"](https://reddotblog.com/showing-your-art-in-cafes-restaurants-banks-and-other-venues-2/) is the best single evidence source:
- **No sales mechanism.** One artist discovered after a month-long exhibit that a regular patron didn't know the work was for sale; servers couldn't name the artist or confirm pieces were purchasable. Staff have no incentive or training to sell.
- **Damage and environment.** Paintings absorbing kitchen smoke/odours for six months; coffee-steam damage at a Starbucks; frames greased in restaurant-bar settings; work hung at chair height getting knocked.
- **Insurance gap.** Work typically hangs entirely at the artist's risk; neither party insures it.
- **Time cost.** Installation/takedown is unpaid labour; marketing collateral (cards, statements) goes missing.
- **Highly variable outcomes.** The same thread contains a $7,000+ café show in Seattle and three-originals-plus-eleven-prints in six months at a seafood restaurant — alongside 16 years of medical-office displays with minimal sales and month-long shows with zero. The consensus advice: treat cafés as exposure and "visible storage," run an opening event, incentivise staff, and expect sales only if you actively drive them.
- **Admin friction (AU-specific).** If the artist is GST-registered the sale price includes GST and the artist must issue a compliant tax invoice; GST may also apply on the venue's commission ([NAVA](https://code.visualarts.net.au/selling-and-marketing/public-galleries-selling-work/detailed-discussion)) — paperwork most cafés and hobby-to-emerging artists simply don't do, which is why cash-and-a-handshake persists.

**Pain points (venue side).** Work that stays too long and goes stale; awkwardness chasing artists to collect unsold work; wall damage disputes; staff fielding price questions; no upside unless they impose a commission they then have to administer. Written contracts are the exception, not the rule ([WetCanvas](https://www.wetcanvas.com/forums/topic/commision-on-art-in-local-shop/)).

**The AccessArt read:** every one of these frictions is a platform feature — QR sale flow removes staff from the transaction, scheduled rotation prevents staleness, standard digital agreement + platform-handled invoicing/GST removes admin, and a damage policy/insurance layer addresses the biggest trust gap.

## 3. What's in it for the venue

- **Differentiation and atmosphere.** Hospitality-marketing sources consistently frame rotating local art as a low-cost way to differentiate, generate conversation and social content, and signal community roots ([7shifts coffee-shop marketing guide](https://www.7shifts.com/blog/coffee-shop-ideas/), [Grafterr on community events driving café foot traffic](https://www.grafterr.com/blog/leveraging-community-events-to-drive-cafe-foot-traffic/)).
- **Event-driven traffic is the proven mechanism.** The hard evidence for art driving hospitality traffic attaches to *events* (openings, art-walk nights) more than static displays: Colorado Springs' First Friday Art Walk drew **22,000+ attendees across 12 events and 42 businesses in 2025** ([KOAA](https://www.koaa.com/news/local-news/in-your-community/central-springs/colorado-springs-first-friday-art-walk-draws-thousands-and-keeps-downtown-galleries-alive)), and Asheville relaunched its First Friday walk in 2026 explicitly to "boost foot traffic" for downtown creative businesses ([WLOS](https://wlos.com/news/local/first-friday-art-walk-asheville-downtown-arts-district-relaunches-2026-season-asheville-galleries-creative-businesses-nonprofit-community-organization-foot-traffic-economic-recovery-helene-covid)). RedDotBlog commenters likewise report sales clustering around opening receptions, not passive display.
- **Quantified static-display claims are weak.** Figures circulating in café-marketing blogs — "up to 20% more foot traffic from featuring local artists," "customers stay 30 minutes longer with rotating exhibitions" ([coffeeshops-nearme.com](https://coffeeshops-nearme.com/cafes-featuring-local-artists-and-exhibitions-where-coffee-meets-creativity/)) — **come from unsourced content-marketing pages and should be treated as unverified**. Don't put them in a venue pitch deck; use the event-attendance numbers and the revenue-share instead.
- **The revenue-share angle is genuinely novel for venues.** Under Artwalls' model a venue earns 15% of sales for zero cost ([Artwalls](https://artwalls.space/)); under the informal status quo most Australian cafés earn nothing from their walls. Against a backdrop where **~1 in 8 Australian cafés/restaurants closed in the past year (12%, vs 5.4% economy-wide)** and hospitality leads all sectors for payment defaults ([CreditorWatch](https://creditorwatch.com.au/blog/one-in-eight-cafes-restaurants-closing), [CreditorWatch outlook](https://creditorwatch.com.au/blog/tough-start-to-2025-ahead-for-australian-businesses-sector-outlook-worst-for-hospitality)), "free rotating décor + a new passive revenue line + inclusion in a promoted art trail" is a pitch tuned to the moment.

## 4. Art trails and tours as a discovery mechanism

**First Friday art walks (US model).** Monthly, evening, precinct-based; galleries *and ordinary businesses* stay open with exhibitions/performances. Organisation is typically a small arts nonprofit or downtown association: Phoenix's founding model was nonprofit Artlink ([Visit Phoenix](https://www.visitphoenix.com/things-to-do/arts-culture/first-friday-art-walk/)); Asheville's 2026 relaunch is run by a new Downtown Arts Association ([WLOS](https://wlos.com/news/local/first-friday-art-walk-asheville-downtown-arts-district-relaunches-2026-season-asheville-galleries-creative-businesses-nonprofit-community-organization-foot-traffic-economic-recovery-helene-covid)); Boulder's went citywide in Aug 2026 via arts nonprofit The New Local with the NoBo Art District and Chamber ([Boulder Reporting Lab](https://boulderreportinglab.org/2026/08/06/boulders-first-friday-art-walks-are-going-citywide/)); Eugene's is run by the Lane Arts Council ([lanearts.org](https://lanearts.org/first-friday-artwalk/)). Funding: downtown improvement boards and local business sponsors (Pensacola's sponsors fund a free trolley — [Ballinger Publishing](https://ballingerpublishing.com/first-fridays-art-walk/)). Key numbers: Colorado Springs 22,000 attendees / 42 businesses / 2025 ([KOAA](https://www.koaa.com/news/local-news/in-your-community/central-springs/colorado-springs-first-friday-art-walk-draws-thousands-and-keeps-downtown-galleries-alive)). **No Brisbane equivalent currently runs at precinct scale — an open lane for AccessArt.**

**Brisbane Art Design (BAD).** Museum of Brisbane's biennial-style festival: **150+ events and exhibitions across 25+ venues** in the CBD, West End, Fortitude Valley, South Brisbane, Woolloongabba and beyond, with MoB as hub and satellite precinct programming ([Museum of Brisbane](https://www.museumofbrisbane.com.au/about-museum-of-brisbane/programs-initiatives/brisbane-art-design/), [Must Do Brisbane](https://www.mustdobrisbane.com/archives/brisbane-art-design-bad-2023-brisbane)). Demonstrates institutional appetite for multi-venue art trails in Brisbane and a natural partnership/aggregation target.

**Brisbane Open House.** Free annual weekend; **93 buildings in 2026**, mostly free self-guided entry ([BCC listing](https://www.brisbane.qld.gov.au/events/brisbane-open-house-2026/201109864)). Funded by a mix: ~$150,000 Queensland Government funding plus ~$111,500 corporate partnerships and ~$250,000 in-kind (2019 figures) ([Arts Queensland case study](https://www.arts.qld.gov.au/case-studies/brisbane-open-house)). The template: government seed + sponsor money + venues opting in free because participation is its own marketing.

**Regional Queensland art trails.** The [Granite Belt Art & Craft Trail](https://www.gbart.org.au/site/street-art.html) is a self-guided, self-drive open-studios trail from Dalveen to the NSW border, run through the local art association with artists exhibiting in studios, galleries *and community spaces*, cross-promoted with wineries and cafés ([Southern Downs & Granite Belt tourism](https://southerndownsandgranitebelt.com.au/events/classes-lessons-workshops-and-talks/granite-belt-art-and-craft-trail/)). The separate Stanthorpe (street) Art Trail is navigated via the council's **official Southern Downs Visitor App** ([Stanthorpe Art Trail](https://southerndownsandgranitebelt.com.au/attractions/galleries-museums-and-collections/stanthorpe-art-trail/)) — councils here already fund trail apps. Scenic Rim Regional Council runs arts grant rounds for community creative projects ([Scenic Rim](https://www.scenicrim.qld.gov.au/Our-Council/Connect-with-Council/2025-News/Arts-grants-create-opportunities-to-bring-big-ideas-to-life)). SWELL Sculpture Festival (Currumbin, Gold Coast) is SEQ's biggest outdoor sculpture trail, 65+ works with published maps ([SWELL](https://www.swellsculpture.com.au/swell-map/)).

**App-driven trails already exist in Australia.** The [Silo Art Australia app](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.AustralianArtTrails.ios&hl=en-US) maps the national silo art trail with directions and artist info; City of Sydney's [Sydney Culture Walks app](https://www.sydneyculturewalksapp.com/) delivers self-guided public-art routes; and closest to home, **[SEQ Food Trails](https://seqfoodtrails.com.au/) was commissioned by 13 SEQ councils via the Council of Mayors (SEQ)**, offering businesses free listings and an interactive mapping tool for personalised trails across Brisbane, Logan, Moreton Bay, Redlands, Scenic Rim, Toowoomba and the Sunshine Coast ([Good Design Award](https://good-design.org/projects/south-east-queensland-food-trails/), [Lockyer Valley RC](https://www.lockyervalley.qld.gov.au/our-council/news?item=id:2ecm7lnpc17q9sagw78g)). **An "SEQ Art Trails" layer of participating cafés/venues is a direct, fundable analogue — and the Council of Mayors precedent is the door to knock on.** Brisbane's Paddington Art Trail (galleries + cafés along Latrobe/Given Tce) already functions informally as a precinct art crawl ([eatdrinkandbekerry](https://eatdrinkandbekerry.net/index.php/2021/07/13/what-you-must-see-on-the-paddington-art-trail/)).

## 5. Tech patterns: QR-on-the-wall → artwork page → buy

- **The pattern is established, the integration isn't.** QR labels linking wall art to an online purchase page are standard advice for artists and galleries ([QR Code Generator guide](https://www.qr-code-generator.com/qr-codes-on/art/), [Paired Inc: QR codes for artists and galleries](https://www.pairedinc.com/blog/qr-codes-for-artists-and-galleries/), [QR Planet](https://qrplanet.com/qr-codes-for-artists-and-art)); Art Storefronts ships auto-generated QR codes that deep-link to each artist's own store ([Art Storefronts](https://blog.artstorefronts.com/qr-codes-for-artists/)). Vooglue sells smart QR art labels where the buyer sees a buy button and pays "through a secure checkout powered by Stripe" without staff involvement ([Vooglue](https://vooglue.com/art-labels-or-didactics-with-a-smart-art-qr-code-and-an-art-thumbnail/)). Artists on consignment in host businesses actively ask for exactly this tooling ([Ask MetaFilter thread](https://ask.metafilter.com/373951/QR-codes-for-selling-on-consignment-pop-up-hosted-in-another-buisiness)).
- **Artwalls proves the end-to-end venue flow**: QR beside the piece → artwork page → Stripe checkout, with automatic three-way split to artist, venue and platform ([Artwalls](https://artwalls.space/)). This is the reference architecture: Stripe Connect-style destination charges with application fees map cleanly onto artist-85 / venue-15 / platform-fee splits.
- **Smartify shows the discovery/tour layer.** Smartify ("Shazam for art") does image-recognition scanning, artwork pages, audio guides and in-app shop across major museums ([Yale University Art Gallery](https://artgallery.yale.edu/news/smartify-gallery), [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2018/01/smartify-a-shazam-for-art-lets-you-use-your-phone-to-scan-identify-learn-about-major-works-of-art.html)). It's museum-centric, not a café sales tool — but its scan → story → shop flow is the UX benchmark for AccessArt's trail app.
- **Practical notes from the field**: use dynamic QR codes (relink when the piece rotates without reprinting), one code per artwork not per wall, physical durability matters (laminated/framed labels in food environments), and the artwork page must show price, artist story, and a "see more nearby" hook to feed the trail. NFC is a nice-to-have; QR is universal. No evidence was found of anyone in Australia running QR-to-Stripe purchase off café walls at platform scale — flagging this as an apparent gap rather than a verified absence.

## 6. The commercial/office placement market (adjacent revenue)

Australia already has a functioning paid art-rental market that AccessArt's emerging-artist inventory can undercut on price and beat on story:

- **[Artbank](https://www.artbank.gov.au/art-leasing-business-0)** (government): $165–$11,000/artwork/yr, $550 minimum, tax-deductible — the price anchor.
- **[Corporate Art Rentals](https://corporateartrentals.com.au/)** (Melbourne): long-term corporate rentals, **rotating works every six months** for tenant/client freshness.
- **[Wentworth Galleries](https://www.wentworthgalleries.com.au/art-rental)** (Sydney): 6–36-month rentals with purchase option, serving corporates in most capitals including Brisbane.
- **[Studio Gallery Group](https://www.studiogallerymelbourne.com.au/pages/art-rental)**: leases from 1 day to 36 months, marketing "100% tax-deductible" hard; also [Art+](https://art-plus.com.au/art-leasing/) and [Art Index](https://www.artindex.com.au/corporate-art-rental).
- **US benchmark:** TurningArt's subscription-rotation model and 60%/25% artist economics (Section 1); ArtLifting's corporate-partnership pivot shows B2B is where social-impact art placement found sustainable revenue ([ArtLifting blog](https://www.artlifting.com/blogs/uplifting/the-reason-behind-artliftings-investment-in-corporate-partnerships)).

Target segments for AccessArt: offices/coworking, medical and allied-health waiting rooms, hotels (Art Pharmacy already does hotel commissions in AU — [example](https://www.artpharmacy.com.au/projects/hotel-art-commission)), and build-to-rent/lobby placemaking (Art Pharmacy's lobby activations show developer appetite — [project](https://artpharmacy.com.au/projects/digital-art-activation-in-sydney-lobby/)). The commercial side pays monthly subscription fees (predictable revenue, tax-deductible for the client) while the café side pays per-sale commission — the classic two-sided stabiliser. No dollar-size estimate for the AU corporate art-rental market was found from a reliable source; treat market size as unquantified.

## 7. Key takeaways for AccessArt

1. **Venue deal structure:** free for venues, always (Artwalls-proven), with a **10–15% venue share of sales** — it converts the wall from free décor into a passive revenue line, beats the informal 0% status quo for venues without gouging artists, and keeps the artist take at 60–75% after a 15–25% platform commission (well above gallery norms, in line with Artwalls/TurningArt/Café Art).
2. **Platform absorbs the friction that kills the informal model:** standard digital display agreement, scheduled rotation (6–12 weeks; Corporate Art Rentals uses 6-monthly for offices), platform-issued invoices/GST handling per NAVA guidance, hanging kit + guidelines (nothing at chair height, away from steam/grease), and a stated damage/insurance policy — the single biggest trust gap in forum evidence.
3. **QR → artwork page → Stripe is validated, not novel — speed matters.** Artwalls (San Diego) is already executing the exact model; first-mover advantage in SEQ is real but perishable.
4. **Subsidise the artist side's onboarding, but the venue side is the scarcer asset.** Artists already give this labour away for free (forum evidence); venues under margin pressure need a zero-cost, zero-effort pitch. Sign marquee venues in 2–3 walkable precincts (Paddington, West End, Fortitude Valley) before widening — trail density is the product.
5. **Launch the trail as a monthly First-Friday-style event, not just a map.** Sales cluster around events (RedDotBlog evidence; 22k attendees across 42 businesses in Colorado Springs). A monthly "AccessArt Friday" per precinct gives venues a traffic story and artists a sales spike; the app map makes it self-guided the other 29 days.
6. **Chase council/tourism money early.** SEQ Food Trails (13 councils via Council of Mayors SEQ), Brisbane Open House (~$150k state funding + sponsors), Southern Downs' trail app, and Scenic Rim arts grants all prove SEQ councils fund exactly this trail infrastructure. Pitch "SEQ Art Trails" as the Food Trails sibling; time it to BAD festival for launch amplification.
7. **Risks to engineer around:** *venue churn* (12% annual café/restaurant failure rate — keep artwork custody light, make de-listing/retrieval a one-tap workflow, over-recruit venues); *damage/theft* (placement rules, condition photos at install, small insurance pool or damage waiver funded from platform fee); *Café Art's lesson* that a café-only model is fragile — the corporate/office subscription side (Section 6) is the revenue ballast.
8. **Don't oversell foot-traffic stats.** The circulating "20% more traffic / 30-minute longer dwell" figures are unsourced blog claims; sell venues on zero cost + revenue share + trail marketing + event nights, using verifiable event-attendance numbers.
9. **Position against the gap the AU incumbents left:** Art Pharmacy and Curatorial+Co. went upmarket to curated B2B; Artbank and rental firms serve corporates with established artists. Nobody serves emerging artists × hospitality walls × consumer QR sales in Australia — that's the wedge, with corporate placement as the follow-on.
